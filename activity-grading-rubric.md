# Grading Rubric for Interactive AI Activities

*Introduction to Databases — Marshall University*

## How Your Conversations Will Be Graded

At the end of each chapter, you complete Interactive Activities by having a conversation with an AI tool and submitting the transcript. This rubric explains exactly how those transcripts are graded.

Instead of vague categories like "good participation," this rubric is a checklist of specific **yes/no questions** about your transcript. Each question can be answered by pointing at evidence in the conversation itself. Your grade is the percentage of applicable items marked "Yes."

This design follows a simple principle: every criterion should be so concrete that two different graders — human or AI — reading your transcript would reach the same answer. There is no partial credit on individual items and no judgment calls about "quality" in the abstract. The transcript either shows the behavior or it doesn't.

A few things to know before you start:

- **You are not graded on getting every answer right on the first try.** Being wrong and then getting it right is what studying looks like, and this rubric rewards it. What costs you points is not trying, not explaining your reasoning, and not engaging with feedback.
- **Your first attempt is what counts as "your answer."** The grader looks at what you said *before* the AI gave feedback, so asking the AI to answer for you doesn't earn mastery credit — it removes it.
- **Submit the complete transcript**, from the moment you paste the activity prompt through the AI's closing summary.

## Scoring

Each item below is scored **Yes (1)** or **No (0)**. Items that don't apply to a particular activity are skipped and don't count for or against you.

**Score = (number of Yes items) ÷ (number of applicable items)**

| Score | Level |
|---|---|
| 90–100% | Excellent |
| 75–89% | Proficient |
| 60–74% | Developing |
| Below 60% | Beginning |

## Section 1 — Mastery of the Topic

These items measure whether your **first-attempt answers** — before the AI corrected anything — show you understand the chapter.

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | The student attempts a substantive answer to every question the AI asks (no skipped questions, no "I don't know" left standing without a follow-up attempt). | |
| 1.2 | At least three-quarters of the student's first-attempt answers are correct or mostly correct before any AI feedback. | |
| 1.3 | The student uses key terms from the chapter (not just everyday paraphrases) in their answers. | |
| 1.4 | Every time the student uses a technical term from the chapter, they use it correctly. | |
| 1.5 | When the AI corrects a misunderstanding, the student's later answers reflect the correction (the same error does not appear again in the transcript). | |
| 1.6 | At least one answer connects a specific concept to the chapter's bigger idea (e.g., why structure matters, why integrity rules exist, "default to relational; deviate with a reason"). | |
| 1.7 | The student produces at least one correct example of their own that does not appear in the chapter or in the AI's messages. | |

---

## Section 2 — Depth of Engagement

These items measure whether you treated the activity as a real study session or raced through it. They distinguish shallow engagement (minimal answers, ignoring feedback, declining every offer to go deeper) from deep engagement (reasoning, follow-ups, connections to your own experience).

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1 | The student's answers are typically complete thoughts (a sentence or more), not one-word or single-phrase responses. | |
| 2.2 | In at least half of their answers, the student explains *why* — giving reasoning, not just a final answer or label. | |
| 2.3 | The student accepts at least one of the AI's offers to "go deeper," OR asks at least one follow-up question of their own. | |
| 2.4 | The student responds to at least one piece of AI feedback with more than acknowledgment — asking a clarifying question, restating the idea in their own words, pushing back, or testing it against a new example. | |
| 2.5 | At least once, the student expresses uncertainty, makes a prediction before answering, or revises their own thinking mid-conversation (e.g., "wait, actually I think it's..."). | |
| 2.6 | Where the activity invites it, the student brings in a specific example from their own life or interests (an app they use, music they listen to, a job they've had) rather than a generic or hypothetical one. | |
| 2.7 | The student completes the activity's closing task (final synthesis, generating their own examples, choosing the hardest scenario, etc.) with a substantive response. | |

## Section 3 — Adaptive Criteria by Activity Type

Each chapter has four activity types. The grader applies **only the block matching the activity submitted**, in addition to Sections 1–2.

### 3A — Concept Check (Activity x.1)

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 3A.1 | The student's definitions capture the *meaning* of each concept, not just memorized wording. | |
| 3A.2 | When asked for examples, the student supplies concrete, specific examples (not restatements of the definition). | |
| 3A.3 | When the student's answer is incomplete, they engage with the AI's completion (rather than simply typing "ok" and moving on) at least once. | |

### 3B — Apply It (Activity x.2)

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 3B.1 | The student chooses a specific, real system or example (a named app, a real scenario) rather than an invented or vague one. | |
| 3B.2 | The student's answers are specific to their chosen example — details in the answers could only apply to that example, not to any generic case. | |
| 3B.3 | The student's answers correctly apply the chapter's concepts to the example (the concepts are used to analyze, not just mentioned). | |
| 3B.4 | The student identifies at least one trade-off, limitation, or blind spot in their own analysis or design. | |

### 3C — Practice / Classification Drill (Activity x.3)

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 3C.1 | The student states their reasoning for every classification or judgment *before* seeing the AI's feedback. | |
| 3C.2 | The student's reasoning cites the relevant property or rule (e.g., "titles aren't unique," "this violates referential integrity"), not just intuition ("it feels like knowledge"). | |
| 3C.3 | When the student misclassifies an item, their explanation after feedback shows they understand *why* the correct answer is correct. | |
| 3C.4 | The student's self-generated examples at the end are original and correctly constructed. | |

### 3D — Case Study / Scenario Roleplay (Activity x.4)

| # | Criterion | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 3D.1 | The student stays in the scenario, responding to the AI's role (manager, supervisor, client) as a participant rather than answering abstractly. | |
| 3D.2 | The student's diagnoses name the specific chapter concept at issue (e.g., which integrity rule is violated, what data-quality problem is occurring). | |
| 3D.3 | The student proposes concrete, actionable solutions — steps someone could actually take — not just "fix the data" or "be more careful." | |
| 3D.4 | At least one of the student's answers addresses *prevention* or root cause, not just cleanup of the immediate problem. | |

## Instructions for the AI Grader

*(Students: this section is included so you can see exactly how the automated grading works. Nothing here is hidden from you.)*

You are grading a transcript of a student's conversation with an AI study tool against the rubric above. Follow this procedure exactly.

1. **Identify the activity type.** Read the pasted prompt at the start of the transcript and classify it as Concept Check (3A), Apply It (3B), Practice/Classification (3C), or Case Study/Roleplay (3D). Apply Sections 1, 2, and only the matching Section 3 block.

2. **Filter for applicability.** Before scoring, mark any item that cannot apply to this transcript as N/A and exclude it from the denominator. (Example: 2.6 is N/A for a Concept Check with no invitation to use personal examples.) List N/A items and a one-line reason for each.

3. **Score each remaining item Yes or No.** For every item, quote the specific line(s) of the transcript that justify your answer. If you cannot point to evidence, the answer is "No." Attribute turns carefully: only text written by the student counts as the student's answer, and only text appearing *before* the AI's feedback on that question counts as a first attempt.

4. **Compute and report.** Output a table of all applicable items with Yes/No and evidence, the score as (Yes count) / (applicable count) and a percentage, the corresponding level, and a 2–3 sentence narrative summary written *to the student* that names one specific strength and one specific way to improve their next session.

5. **Be conservative.** When evidence is ambiguous, score "No" and say why in the evidence column. Never infer effort or understanding that isn't visible in the transcript.
